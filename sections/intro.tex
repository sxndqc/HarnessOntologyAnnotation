\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Annotating text with a rich ontology takes two things. One is an understanding of the structure being annotated: what an event is, how its participants and time are related, what counts as the same entity. The other is the given codebook, the guideline that fixes the labels, the conventions and the hard cases, and that annotators apply rather than invent \citep{halterman2026what,pustejovsky2012natural}. Complex ontologies such as Uniform Meaning Representation \citep[UMR;][]{vangysel2021designing,bonn2024building} and TimeML \citep{pustejovsky2003timeml,sauri2006timeml} are hardest to annotate at the beginning, when there is little annotated data. With enough data a parser can be trained; the systems of the first UMR parsing shared task are fine-tuned models \citep{stepanek2026first,heinecke2026orange,devergnette2026sema}. With a few annotated sentences that route is closed. Large language models (LLMs) offer a way to bootstrap: pretraining gives them a working understanding of meaning and of structured formats (PENMAN graphs, inline XML), and they can be given the guideline itself.

Earlier work has prepared this route. LLMs label text well when given task descriptions or codebooks \citep{gilardi2023chatgpt,he2024annollm,halterman2026codebook}, reasoning models can follow annotation guidelines, and an LLM can keep improving a guideline from its own errors against gold \citep{kim2026refining,kim2026diziner,bibal2025automating}. These refinement studies, however, use simple and direct tasks such as named-entity recognition, whose guidelines fit in one prompt. Bootstrapping a complex scheme is different. The UMR guideline runs to 366 thousand characters, and annotating also needs outside resources such as PropBank frames \citep{palmer2005propbank}. A model given only the long guideline cannot follow all of it: information in the middle of a long context is used poorly \citep{liu2024lost}, accuracy falls as inputs grow \citep{levy2024same} and as instructions accumulate \citep{jaroslawicz2025how}, and agents lose rule details of long procedure manuals \citep{panavas2026handbook}.

We argue that bootstrapping a complex annotation needs more than a better guideline: the guideline has to be broken into a chain of steps, and the steps need tools, so that the model's own abilities are used where they are strong and supported where they are not. We present \sys (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}), which builds an annotator from the guideline and a few annotated sentences by recursive self-improvement (RSI) of its \emph{harness}: the instructions of each step (skills), the steps themselves and the tools they call. A builder model writes a seed harness from the guideline alone; then, five sentences at a time, it annotates gold sentences with the current harness, summarizes the errors, reflects on their causes and proposes edits, new steps and new tools, which are admitted by machine checks. The result annotates from instructions and a handful of examples, with no fine-tuning.

We evaluate \sys on Chinese and English UMR, using the test documents of the UMR parsing shared task \citep{stepanek2026first}, and on TimeML with the TempEval-3 platinum test set \citep{uzzaman2013semeval}, with Claude Haiku~5.5 and GPT-6.1-sol as annotators and Claude Sonnet~5.5 as builder. Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item A guideline-and-harness optimization method for bootstrapping complex annotation: skills, steps and tools are all editable, and a fine-grained judge tells the builder which part of the scheme each error belongs to (\S\ref{sec:method}).
  \item Evidence that it works without fine-tuning: from ten groups of five annotated sentences, the learned harness improves over the seed on held-out documents in all three settings, \resultHeadline{} (\S\ref{sec:results}).
  \item Evidence of continued evolution: improvements learned on one group carry over to unseen documents, the builder invents tools such as PropBank lookups and new checking steps when told only that resource files exist, and the gains appear in the scheme categories the edits target (\S\ref{sec:how}).
\end{itemize}
