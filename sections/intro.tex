\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Annotating a complex ontology requires both an understanding of its structures and adherence to a given codebook. An annotator must recognize events, participants, and relations, then express them according to the project's definitions and conventions \citep{pustejovsky2012natural,kim2026refining}. This is hardest at the start of an annotation project, when examples are scarce: with enough annotated data a parser can be trained, and with a few annotated sentences that route is closed. Large language models (LLMs) offer a way to bootstrap: pretraining gives them a working understanding of linguistic structures and familiarity with structured output formats. Supplying the guideline can direct these capabilities toward the intended annotation scheme.

Prior work has paved this route. \citet{kim2026refining} show that reasoning models can apply annotation guidelines and that an iterative moderation process can improve the guidelines from annotation errors, on named-entity recognition, a flat labeling task. Bootstrapping a complex ontology makes two further demands. Guidelines can be lengthy: the TimeML manual spans 71 pages \citep{sauri2006timeml}; annotation may also require external resources, such as PropBank frames for Uniform Meaning Representation (UMR) \citep{vangysel2021designing,palmer2005propbank}. Models follow long guidelines unevenly: information in the middle of a long context is used poorly \citep{liu2024lost}, and agents lose details of long procedural instructions \citep{panavas2026handbook}.

We propose decomposing the guideline into a chain of steps and equipping those steps with tools, then improving this \emph{harness} through recursive self-improvement (RSI). We call this approach \sys (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). A builder LLM derives a seed harness from the guideline; fine-grained feedback on a few annotated examples then guides revisions to step instructions, the workflow, and tools. The harness organizes the model's linguistic judgments and supports them with resource lookups and executable rules. It thus turns instructions and limited supervision into a working annotator, without fine-tuning.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item A guideline-and-harness optimization framework for bootstrapping complex annotation, in which instructions, steps, and tools are all open to refinement (\S\ref{sec:method}).
  \item Evidence that an annotator bootstrapped from a guideline and 50 annotated sentences exceeds the best fine-tuned shared-task system on the same UMR test documents, and that the harness contributes more than the choice of annotating model: editing the guideline corrects notation, while the step decomposition improves the graphs (\S\ref{sec:main}, \S\ref{sec:controls}).
  \item Evidence of continued evolution: harness improvements transfer to unseen texts and other annotators, while the builder creates and revises resource lookup tools and turns recurring errors into rules (\S\ref{sec:general}, \S\ref{sec:how}).
\end{enumerate}
