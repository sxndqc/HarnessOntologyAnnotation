\subsection{Main results}
\label{sec:main}

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives every system on the same test documents.

\paragraph{A long guideline in the prompt is not enough.} Given the whole guideline and the sentence, Haiku reaches \mainBaseHaikuZh{} AnCast++ F on Chinese UMR and Sonnet \mainBaseSonnetZh{}; on English the prompt baselines are stronger (\mainBaseHaikuEn{} and \mainBaseSonnetEn{}). Their errors follow the guideline's own notation where the data use another: modal strength written as \texttt{:modstr FullAff} inside the graph instead of document-level relations such as \texttt{(author :full-affirmative e)}, capitalized attribute values, \texttt{:ref-number} for \texttt{:refer-number}. Every prompt baseline has a document-level modal score of 0.

\paragraph{The seed harness helps where the guideline is hard to apply.} Written from the same guideline, the three-step seed improves over the Haiku prompt baseline on Chinese UMR (\mainSeedZh{} vs.\ \mainBaseHaikuZh{}) and on TimeML (TLINK \mainSeedTml{} vs.\ \mainBaseHaikuTml{}), where it is above the best TempEval-3 participant on our documents (ClearTK-2, \sotaTlinkCleartk{}) and below the organizers' TIPSem (\sotaTlinkTipsem{}). On English UMR the seed is below the prompt baseline (\mainSeedEn{} vs.\ \mainBaseHaikuEn{}): a one-time compression of the guideline into 18K characters of checklists loses detail.

\paragraph{RSI improves the harness on held-out documents.} After ten rounds of five sentences each, $H_{10}$ improves over the seed on both UMR test sets: Chinese F \mainSeedZh{}~$\rightarrow$~\mainLearnedZh{} (paired document bootstrap \gainLearnedZh{}, 95\% interval \gainLearnedZhLo{} to \gainLearnedZhHi{}; sentence level \sentLearnedZh{}, \sentLearnedZhBetter{} of \sentLearnedZhN{} sentences better and \sentLearnedZhWorse{} worse by more than a point) and English \mainSeedEn{}~$\rightarrow$~\mainLearnedEn{} (\gainLearnedEn{}, \gainLearnedEnLo{} to \gainLearnedEnHi{}; \sentLearnedEnBetter{} of \sentLearnedEnN{} better, \sentLearnedEnWorse{} worse). The comprehensive score, which adds the document level, rises more (\compSeedZh{}~$\rightarrow$~\compLearnedZh{}; \compSeedEn{}~$\rightarrow$~\compLearnedEn{}), and so does the official \jumach{} score against the shared task's own gold (\jumachSeedZh{}~$\rightarrow$~\jumachLearnedZh{}; \jumachSeedEn{}~$\rightarrow$~\jumachLearnedEn{}). $H_{10}$ is above the best shared-task system on the same documents on every UMR metric (\jumach{} \sotaJumachZh{} and \sotaJumachEn{}; F \sotaFZh{} and \sotaFEn{}), although that system was fine-tuned on the shared task's training data and we used 50 annotated sentences. Two independent repetitions of $H_0$ and $H_{10}$ with fresh samples differ by \repDiffSeedZh{}/\repDiffLearnedZh{} (Chinese) and \repDiffSeedEn{}/\repDiffLearnedEn{} (English) F, well below the gains. On TimeML, temporal awareness does not improve (\mainSeedTml{}~$\rightarrow$~\mainLearnedTml{}); \S\ref{sec:how} shows what does change.

\subsection{Guideline or harness?}
\label{sec:controls}

\input{tables/controls}

Is the harness needed, or would a better guideline do? Table~\ref{tab:controls} compares, with Haiku in every role, ways of giving the model the guideline.

\paragraph{Reading the guideline through tools is worse than having it in the prompt.} With only the guideline's address and tools to search and read it, Haiku falls below the prompt baseline everywhere, most on Chinese (F \ctlToolsZh{} vs.\ \ctlPromptZh{}). It does look things up (\ctlToolUseZh\%, \ctlToolUseEn\% and \ctlToolUseTml\% of its calls use a tool), but it looks up what it knows it needs, not the conventions it does not know it is missing: on Chinese it writes English concepts (\texttt{define-01}, \texttt{spaceflight}) for Chinese words. A stronger reasoning model reads better: on the two documents per benchmark of Appendix Table~\ref{tab:transfer}, GPT-6.1-sol with tools reaches \subGptToolsZh{} (Chinese) and \subGptToolsEn{} (English) F, as high as $H_{10}$ with Haiku (\subLearnedHHZh{}, \subLearnedHHEn{}) or with GPT (\subLearnedGGZh{}, \subLearnedGGEn{}) on the same documents; the harness keeps its lead on the Chinese document level and on the official score (comprehensive \subGptToolsCompZh{} vs.\ \subLearnedHHCompZh{}, \jumach{} \subGptToolsJumachZh{} vs.\ \subLearnedHHJumachZh{}).

\paragraph{Editing the guideline fixes conventions, not structure.} One round of reflection that may only edit the guideline prompt appends a block of ``house conventions'' to it (4--7K characters, at the end or right after the first line of the 366K-character guideline). This is what the harness's first round also learns, and it pays where conventions are the problem: \jumach{}, which scores alignment and notation, rises from \ctlPromptJumachZh{} to \ctlGuideOneJumachZh{} on Chinese and from \ctlPromptJumachEn{} to \ctlGuideOneJumachEn{} on English, and the comprehensive score rises on both. On English, where the prompt baseline is already strong, F reaches \ctlGuideOneEn{}, as high as $H_{10}$ (\ctlHarnessTenEn{}). On Chinese, F barely moves (\ctlPromptZh{}~$\rightarrow$~\ctlGuideOneZh{}) while the harness after the same round is at \ctlHarnessOneZh{}, and on TimeML the edited guideline (TLINK \ctlGuideOneTml{}) stays below the seed harness (\ctlSeedTml{}). The difference is the decomposition: a guideline edit can say what the data's notation is, but not split the work into a draft, a check against the guideline and a revision, or give the model a lookup. Where the model's single pass is already good (English), conventions are most of what is missing; where it is not (Chinese), the steps are.

\subsection{What RSI learns carries over}
\label{sec:general}

Each round improves its own training group, by \trainGainZh{} (Chinese), \trainGainEn{} (English) and \trainGainTml{} (TimeML) points on average (Figure~\ref{fig:curves}, orange). Fitting five sentences would produce such gains on its own. For UMR they are not only that: the test score rises in most rounds (\roundsTestUpZh{} of 10 for Chinese, \roundsTestUpEn{} for English), and across rounds the training gain and the test gain are positively correlated, though with ten rounds not significantly (Spearman $\rho$ \genRhoZh{} and \genRhoEn{}, $p$ \genRhoPZh{} and \genRhoPEn{}). The test curves rise for three to six groups, 15--30 annotated sentences, and then flatten (best \bestTestZh{} at round \bestRoundZh{} for Chinese, \bestTestEn{} at round \bestRoundEn{} for English), while the training groups keep improving: later rounds increasingly fix what is particular to a group, a narrative, a list of bond prices, a phrasal verb. Since no edit is tested before it is kept, a few rounds lower the test score, by up to two points.

\paragraph{Across models.} $H_{10}$ was built by watching Haiku. Run unchanged with other annotators, it helps them as much: with GPT-6.1-sol as generator and critic, Chinese F rises from \mainSeedGGZh{} (seed) to \mainLearnedGGZh{} (comprehensive and \jumach{} as with Haiku, Table~\ref{tab:main}); on the \subDocsEn{} sentences of the two English documents the GPT run of $H_{10}$ completed within our API budget, from \SubSeedGGEn{} to \SubLearnedGGEn{} (Haiku: \SubSeedHHEn{}~$\rightarrow$~\SubLearnedHHEn{} on the same documents); with Sonnet in both roles, Chinese \sonnetSeedFZh{}~$\rightarrow$~\sonnetLearnedFZh{} and English \sonnetSeedFEn{}~$\rightarrow$~\sonnetLearnedFEn{}. On two documents per benchmark we also ran the mixed pairs (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:transfer}): on UMR every pair gains 7 to 14 F from $H_{10}$, and the four pairs end within five points of each other; on TimeML, whose temporal-awareness score on 22 sentences moves by several points between runs, the pairs neither gain nor lose consistently. What the builder wrote is about the scheme, not about the model it watched.

\paragraph{Generator and critic.} At the seed, the choice of models matters much less than the harness (Table~\ref{tab:seed2x2}): the four Haiku/GPT pairs are within three points of each other on every benchmark, close to the run-to-run variation, while RSI adds 6 to 11. A stronger generator helps a little on Chinese (GPT/GPT \mainSeedGGZh{} vs.\ Haiku/Haiku \mainSeedZh{}); swapping only the critic changes little (Haiku/GPT \mainSeedHGZh{}, GPT/Haiku \mainSeedGHZh{}), and the mixed pairs show no complementarity beyond that. Consistently, the builders' main complaint about the critic was not its model but its instructions: it ``fixed'' correct annotations (\S\ref{sec:case}).

\paragraph{Restricting the loop.} Two rounds of RSI from the same seed with restricted operations (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:shortabl}) agree with the ablation of $H_{10}$. Without rule steps the builder ends close to the full loop (Chinese \sabNoRulesHTwoZh{} vs.\ \sabAllHTwoZh{} F at $H_2$, English \sabNoRulesHTwoEn{} vs.\ \sabAllHTwoEn{}), and on Chinese it writes the PropBank tool one round earlier. Without tools, Chinese falls behind (\sabNoToolsHTwoZh{}), most on the official score, and English does not (\sabNoToolsHTwoEn{}): in the first rounds the builder mostly fixes conventions, which instructions can carry, and the tool pays for itself on Chinese senses. With one run per configuration, differences of one or two points are within the run-to-run variation.

\paragraph{Conventions first, then lookups.} Figure~\ref{fig:judge} breaks the gains down by judge check (Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:judgezh}--\ref{tab:judgetml}). For UMR, the largest gains are on conventions that the guideline states differently from the data or not at all: the document-level format and modal relations (Chinese modal 0~$\rightarrow$~50, English 0~$\rightarrow$~32), attribute names and values, entity concepts, granularity. The next largest are PropBank senses (Chinese 88~$\rightarrow$~94, English 67~$\rightarrow$~80) and core arguments of events (52~$\rightarrow$~63, 42~$\rightarrow$~55). Aspect and coreference change little, and document-level temporal relations stay low (Chinese 3~$\rightarrow$~15, English 5~$\rightarrow$~13). The ablation (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) separates the parts: the learned instructions carry most of the gain (with the seed's instructions in place of the learned ones, F falls by \ablSeedSkillsZhDelta{} and \ablSeedSkillsEnDelta{}); removing the tools hardly changes F (\ablNoToolsZhDelta{}, \ablNoToolsEnDelta{}) but costs exactly the checks the tools serve, senses (Chinese 94~$\rightarrow$~78, English 80~$\rightarrow$~73) and core arguments (63~$\rightarrow$~55, 55~$\rightarrow$~47); removing the rule steps changes little (\ablNoRulesZhDelta{}, \ablNoRulesEnDelta{}): the learned instructions alone already produce most of what the rules enforce. Differences of one or two points are within the run-to-run variation measured above.

\paragraph{TimeML: pairs, not relations.} The fine-grained judge shows what the flat temporal-awareness score hides. The platinum-trained harness links the right pairs much more often: event--document time from 34 to 45, event--event within a sentence from 43 to 55, across sentences from 6 to 12 (pair F); but the relation of a pair linked on both sides gets worse (event--event within a sentence 75~$\rightarrow$~66), and temporal awareness needs both. Which pairs to link is a convention of an annotation project; which relation holds is a semantic judgment. Time expressions and events, already near the 2013 systems at $H_0$, change little.

\paragraph{An explicit link step.}\label{sec:link} The judge locates TimeML's problem in which pairs are linked: the seed links events to the document time that the gold leaves unlinked (123 of its 162 event--DCT links), misses most within-sentence event pairs (it links 63 of 174) and gets almost no pair across sentences right. We therefore wrote, by hand, a fourth step that rebuilds a sentence's links as a sparse backbone (a main event, one anchor per other event, the main event to the previous sentence's main event and to the document time) and a rule that links each time expression to the document time from its normalized value (Table~\ref{tab:link}). Each part on its own makes temporal awareness worse: the rule adds time--DCT links that the gold mostly does not have (only 23 of the 138 time expressions of the platinum documents are linked to the DCT, with no pattern of type or value), and the backbone links many pairs across sentences that the gold does not (\lnkLink{}, and \lnkLinkTwo{} when cross-sentence links are restricted to explicit connectives, against \lnkSeed{}); added to $H_{10}$, the same step lowers it too (\lnkTenLink{}, \lnkTenLinkTwo{}). Given to RSI, however, the step becomes something the builder can tune. In the first round on the platinum training group it rewrote the step after the gold's practice (dependents of a clause to its main event, the main event both to its time expression and to the document time) and reached \lnkLinkRsiOne{}, the best TimeML harness in our experiments (recall \lnkLinkRsiOneR{}), above the ten-round $H_{10}$ (\lnkTen{}); in the second round it reduced the over-linking across sentences and lost part of the gain (\lnkLinkRsiTwo{}). A dedicated step does not encode the right links, but it gives the loop a place to learn them.

\paragraph{The builder learns the gold it sees.} Trained on TimeBank and AQUAINT instead (grey in Figure~\ref{fig:curves}), whose temporal links are much sparser than the platinum gold and where several five-sentence windows have no link at all, the harness learns to link less: TLINK precision rises (\tlinkpSeedTml{}~$\rightarrow$~\altTlinkPLearnedTml{}), recall falls (\tlinkrSeedTml{}~$\rightarrow$~\altTlinkRLearnedTml{}), and temporal awareness drops to \altTlinkLearnedTml{}, while time values improve (\valueSeedTml{}~$\rightarrow$~\altValueLearnedTml{}). The loop does what it is built to do, adapt the annotator to the gold it is given; it cannot tell a project's convention from an accident of a corpus. The builders' suggestions, which the loop never applies, point the same way: 65 of the 75 suggestions of the three main trajectories are about the gold, 34 of them explicitly about inconsistencies (e.g.\ \texttt{:refer-number} on plural nouns in some English documents but not in others), which no learner can resolve and a human moderator should.

\paragraph{Tools are proposed rarely, but they are proposed.} Admission was deliberately lenient: machine checks only, no reviewer. The UMR builders were told only that PropBank files exist, and each wrote a lookup in the first rounds: \texttt{lookup\_frame} over the Chinese frame files (round 2), which also answers ``no frame'' with the UMR convention for such predicates, and \texttt{roleset\_lookup} over the English argument descriptions (round 1), which the builder replaced in round 9 (and refined again in round 10) after noticing that the annotator chose wrong senses from role descriptions alone (the new version reads the XML frames with sense names and falls back from \emph{researchers} to \emph{research}). With $H_{10}$, \toolUseZh\% (Chinese) and \toolUseEn\% (English) of Haiku's calls use the tool. The TimeML builder, whose only resource was the guideline, wrote no tool. Rule steps were added more often (\stepsAppliedZh{} for Chinese, \stepsAppliedEn{} for English): the builders turned recurring convention errors into code that normalizes modal labels and constants, moves modal strength from the graph to the document level, and repairs variables and alignments. No model step was even proposed: when one kind of decision needed its own pass, the builders preferred code, or a new section in an existing skill, to an extra call per sentence. Table~\ref{tab:ops} counts every operation.

\paragraph{Compact by construction.} Most operations modify or add instruction text; when a skill approached the 10{,}000-character budget, the builder consolidated it (\rewritesAppliedZh{} rewrites for Chinese, \rewritesAppliedEn{} for English). The final harnesses have \sizeLastZh{}K (Chinese), \sizeLastEn{}K (English) and \sizeLastTml{}K (TimeML) characters of instructions, against 366K and 115K for the guidelines (Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:roundszh}--\ref{tab:roundstml}).

\subsection{Case study: one trajectory}
\label{sec:case}

The English trajectory shows the order in which the builder worked (Appendix~\ref{app:case}). Round~1 found that the annotator wrote the guideline's notation (\texttt{:ref-number Singular}, modal labels such as \texttt{FullAff}, tense metanodes in temporal relations), rewrote the three skills in the data's notation, and added the PropBank tool. Rounds~2--6 worked on the document level: the \texttt{(root :modal author)} relation, modal relations for every event with aspect, coreference direction for plural pronouns, and rule steps that clean the document-level graph. Rounds~7--10 turned to semantics: aspect cues such as habitual \emph{would}, phrasal verbs as one roleset (\texttt{pick-up-04}), the sense distinctions that led to the new lookup. The critic was a recurring problem: in several rounds the builder found that \skill{critique} had ``fixed'' correct annotations and \skill{finalize} had applied the fixes, and restricted the critic's checklist, in line with the finding that self-correction without an external signal often hurts \citep{huang2024large,kamoi2024when}.
